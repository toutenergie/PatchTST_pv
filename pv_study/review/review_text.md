## 2. Literature review

The review covers 56 works selected from a corpus of 303 references compiled for this thesis. They are grouped into five axes, ranging from the physical problem (integrating photovoltaics into the grid) to the methodological tool (patch Transformers). For each axis, we identify what is established and what remains open for the grid under study. Table 1 summarises the 56 works: method, type of validation and contribution to the present study.

### 2.1 Large-scale photovoltaic integration and grid stability

The large-scale integration of inverter-based sources transforms grid dynamics. Shah et al. [1] review the stability challenges specific to large-scale photovoltaics. Kenyon et al. [2] show that asynchronous resources change grid control at the cycle and second time scales, and list the questions that remain open. The decline of synchronous inertia makes the frequency more sensitive to generation losses [3, 4]. Johnson et al. [5] treat rotational inertia as a full component of reliability when the share of variable generation is high. Two recent works published in general-science journals qualify this picture. Smith et al. [6] observe daily oscillations of resilience and a marked degradation during periods of clustered generation, which batteries are not sufficient to correct. Xu et al. [7] estimate at about 45 % the solar integration threshold beyond which, without storage, the probability of catastrophic failures rises. On the control side, the virtual synchronous machine [8] and virtual synchronous generators [9] aim to give inverters the behaviour of a rotating machine. Che et al. [10] summarise the strategies for mitigating variability (storage, flexibility, interconnections, virtual power plants). Yuan et al. [11] quantify, on an island grid, the joint benefit of forecasting and storage.

*Takeaway for our study.* All these works make generation forecasting an operational lever: it precedes the sizing of reserves, unit commitment and storage management. However, they do not address how the forecast itself is built.

### 2.2 The Senegalese grid and the West African context

Senegal has its own literature, mostly devoted to the impact of photovoltaics on grid operation. Abdoulaye et al. [12] analyse three years of frequency measurements: the intermittency of PV plants complicates frequency control and multiplies load shedding. Sarr et al. estimate the maximum PV penetration rate admissible by the SENELEC grid at 17.3 % (MV) and 16.4 % (HV) by 2020 [13]. This rate rises to about 24 % with storage and optimised plant siting [14]. Dieng et al. [15] show that secure operation with 57.7 % to 66.8 % of inverter-based resources remains possible if the generation loss does not exceed 5.2 % of total power. Fall et al. [16] study probabilistically sized reserves. Ba et al. [17] report recurrent congestion on some transmission lines at demand peaks by 2025–2030. Ndiaye et al. [18] quantify the effect of five PV plants (Bokhol, Mbour, Mékhé, Ten Mérina, Kahone) on voltage profiles and losses. Our previous works [19, 20], based on causality tests and principal component analysis, established that photovoltaic generation is among the main determinants of the SENELEC grid frequency. Bloomfield et al. [21] describe the Senegalese solar regime as remarkably constant, with maximum wind in the boreal spring. Ndiaye et al. [22] analyse the actual performance and the seasonal forecast of the Diass plant (23 MWp). Coulibaly et al. [56] propose a multilayer perceptron for short-term PV–wind forecasting on the SENELEC grid (R² = 0.89 against 0.85 for linear regression).

*Takeaway for our study.* The Senegalese works rely mostly on grid simulation (PowerFactory, optimal power flow) or statistical analysis. The only predictive contribution [56] uses an MLP without a strict temporal protocol or comparison with persistence. No study forecasts PV generation **aggregated at grid level**, in the presence of successive plant commissionings, with an attention-based model.

### 2.3 Reviews of renewable energy forecasting by machine learning

The reference reviews [23–26, 30] describe the shift from physical and statistical models to deep learning. Wang et al. [23], the most cited synthesis of the corpus, classify the deep architectures applied to renewable energy. Sharifzadeh et al. [25] compare neural networks, SVR and Gaussian processes. Aslam et al. [26] jointly cover load and generation in microgrids. Devaraj et al. [30] report, in the literature they survey, a mean MAPE of 10.29 % for solar. Recent systematic reviews refine this picture. Klaiber and Van Dinther [27] distinguish ten deep approaches across three application domains. Khouili et al. [28] find, over 26 PV forecasting articles, that LSTM (32.7 %) and CNN (28.8 %) dominate, that wavelet decomposition is the most frequent preprocessing, and that the input variables are almost always meteorological (temperature, pressure, humidity). Alazemi et al. [29] conclude that deep networks and ensembles are superior at capturing the autocorrelation of renewable series. Benti et al. [24] and Lin et al. [55] stress the persistent obstacles: uncertainty, data availability, interpretability and the "black box" nature of data-driven models. Liao et al. [52] approach the question through probabilistic modelling in support of stochastic optimisation.

*Takeaway for our study.* Two gaps recur from one review to the next. First, forecasts assume that local meteorological measurements or forecasts are available; second, evaluation protocols are not homogeneous. The stability of performance over time and the inference cost are almost never measured, although they are conditions for adoption by a grid operator.

### 2.4 Deep learning for PV and renewable forecasting

Methodological contributions mainly combine convolutional and recurrent layers. Alsafrani et al. [31] associate a dilated CNN with a bidirectional residual LSTM. Gangwar et al. [32] propose an improved GRU for joint load–generation forecasting. Khan et al. [33] design a lightweight ESN-CNN model to reduce computational complexity. Sankarananth et al. [34] couple neural networks and metaheuristics. Cheng et al. [35] integrate a stability constraint into a spatio-temporal CNN-LSTM framework and report a 14.1 % RMSE reduction relative to LSTM. Abdelsattar et al. [36] compare eight architectures on 4,200 records: TCN comes first (R² = 0.78) and a standard Transformer fails (R² = 0.07). This result indicates that a Transformer cannot be applied as is to a short solar series. Guo et al. [41] address incremental online updating and catastrophic forgetting: it is one of the few works to explicitly consider the drift of performance over time. Li et al. [50] use neural controlled differential equations to reduce the time lag of wind forecasts. Mamyrbayev et al. [51] evaluate their LSTM against persistence (−59.1 % error), a precaution few studies take.

*Takeaway for our study.* The reported gains are rarely measured against a naive baseline, and almost never over an operating horizon of several months without re-training. Yet, for an hourly solar series, daily persistence is a very hard baseline to beat; our study confirms it.

### 2.5 Patch Transformers: PatchTST and its variants

PatchTST [37] introduced two ideas: splitting the series into patches (sub-series) that serve as tokens for the Transformer encoder, and treating each channel independently with shared weights. It markedly improved long-term forecasting. Its transfer to energy is recent and fast. For photovoltaics, Lv et al. [38] combine anomaly detection, GAN-based feature extraction and PatchTST on six plants. Suresh [39] compares five Transformer variants on a 5 kW rooftop with cyclic temporal encodings, and adds adaptive conformal inference: PatchTST achieves the best accuracy, with a coverage rate of 86.2 %. El-kenawy et al. [40] optimise the feature selection and hyper-parameters of PatchTST with a metaheuristic. Lu et al. [42] (CT-PatchTST) explicitly reintroduce **cross-channel** correlations for long-term wind and solar forecasting in Denmark. For wind, Gao et al. [43] add a distribution-adaptive normalisation (Dish-TS). Zhao et al. [44] propose causal sample weighting (−13.3 % RMSE relative to PatchTST alone). Xu et al. [45] couple a PatchTST encoder with a GRU decoder refined by numerical weather prediction. For load, Zhang et al. [46] extend PatchTST to quantile regression, and Spencer et al. [47] show that PatchTST is better suited to transfer learning than the standard Transformer and Informer. Among generic developments, DiffTST [48] improves PatchTST with differential attention. CycleNet [49] explicitly models recurrent cycles with learnable periodic components, which confirms the value of **explicit cyclic information**. On interpretability, Anis et al. [53] apply LIME to stability prediction, and Babakhani et al. [54] combine a Transformer-based mixture of experts with built-in explanations.

*Takeaway for our study.* Three points are agreed upon:
(i) the channel-independence assumption of PatchTST is called into question as soon as informative exogenous variables are available [42, 45];
(ii) periodic information benefits from being provided explicitly [39, 49];
(iii) distribution non-stationarity calls for adaptive normalisation [43].
None of these works, however, uses **deterministic covariates known over the forecast horizon** (solar geometry, future calendar encodings) to condition the forecasting head of PatchTST. Nor does any of them measure the drift of performance over one month of rolling forecasts without re-training.

### 2.6 Positioning of the study

This review brings out five gaps that the present work addresses:

1. **Grid scale.** Forecasts concern a plant or a farm; the aggregated PV generation of a national grid, subject to successive plant commissionings, is not addressed, let alone on a Sahelian grid [12–22, 56].
2. **The absence of meteorological measurements.** Almost all models assume meteorological data [28, 38, 45]. We study what can be achieved without them, using deterministic covariates (cycles and solar geometry) and the grid operating context.
3. **Channel handling.** The contrast between independence (CI) and modelling dependencies between channels (CA) [37, 42] is rarely measured, and never in the case where the future of the covariates is known.
4. **Evaluation under operating conditions.** Comparison with persistence, dispersion over several seeds, robustness to noise, drift over 30 days without re-training and inference cost are rarely brought together in one protocol [24, 41, 51].
5. **Interpretability.** It is cited as an obstacle [24, 55] but rarely measured for Transformers in generation forecasting [53, 54].
